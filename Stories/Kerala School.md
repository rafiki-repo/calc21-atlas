# Kerala School

**Hook:** Around 1400, on India's southwest coast, Madhava found infinite series for π, sine, and cosine, more than two centuries before Europe did. Then a British paper announcing it was ignored for a century.

## The short version

- **Who:** **Madhava of Sangamagrama** (c. 1340-1425; exact dates vary by source), near Kochi in Kerala, founded a teacher-to-student lineage of mathematician-astronomers. Later members include **Nilakantha Somayaji** and **Jyeshthadeva**.
- **What:** series equivalent to the modern power series for **sine**, **cosine**, and **arctangent**. The arctangent series gives π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ..., now usually credited in Europe to Gregory and Leibniz. MacTutor reports that Madhava used **correction terms** to speed it up, getting π to 11 decimal places.
- **The catch:** none of Madhava's own mathematical writings on the series survive. They are known because later texts in the lineage consistently credit him.
- **The proofs:** the **Yuktibhasa** (c. 1530), written by Jyeshthadeva in Malayalam rather than Sanskrit, gives derivations. Physicist-historian P. P. Divakaran titled a 2007 paper on it "The first textbook of calculus".

## Is it calculus?

That depends on what counts.

- **The case for:** K. Ramasubramanian (IIT Bombay), writing for the 2022 International Congress of Mathematicians, argues that the Kerala derivations use the infinitely small, the infinite, and limits, which he calls the three pillars of calculus.
- **The limits of it:** the same article notes that the work stays with the circle and trigonometric functions. European calculus went on to treat arbitrary curves with general rules and notation.

## The neglected paper

**Charles Whish**, an East India Company official in Malabar, described the series in a paper published in 1834, after his death. According to Ramasubramanian, it was largely disregarded for about a century, and some scholars dismissed it as a hoax. Indian mathematicians such as **C. T. Rajagopal** brought the work back into view from the 1940s.

## Did it reach Europe? (claimed, not shown)

- **The hypothesis:** George Gheverghese Joseph and collaborators proposed that Kerala results may have reached Europe through Jesuit missionaries in South India.
- **The archive search:** their own project checked Jesuit-linked archives and, in Joseph's words as quoted in Kim Plofker's review, "yielded no direct evidence". Plofker (2013) adds that there is "a great deal" of evidence for independent rediscovery in Europe.
- **Where that leaves it:** an open conjecture with no documentary support so far. Joseph's wider point, that historians should be clearer about what evidence they demand for a transmission, has been better received.
