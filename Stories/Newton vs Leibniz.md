# Newton vs Leibniz

**Hook:** Two people developed calculus at about the same time. The fight over credit was judged by a committee whose report was secretly written by one of the two.

## The timeline

- **1665-66:** With Cambridge closed by the plague, **Isaac Newton** works out his "method of fluxions" at home. He circulates little and publishes less. His main treatise, written in 1671, appears only in 1736.
- **1675:** **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** has settled on the ∫ notation, still used today.
- **1684 and 1686:** Leibniz **publishes first**, on differential and then integral calculus, in *Acta Eruditorum*. The 1684 paper had no proofs. Jacob Bernoulli called it "an enigma rather than an explanation."
- **1711:** John Keill, writing in the Royal Society's *Transactions*, accuses Leibniz of plagiarism. Leibniz asks the Society to correct the record.
- **1712-13:** The Society, whose president since 1703 was Newton, appoints a committee. It does not ask Leibniz for his account. Its report, published as the *Commercium Epistolicum*, finds for Newton. According to the MacTutor history archive, **Newton wrote it himself**, and then anonymously reviewed it.

## How it is seen now

MacTutor's biographies describe Leibniz's work as an **independent discovery**, and call the committee biased. The two had exchanged letters (via Henry Oldenburg) in 1676-77, which is what Keill's accusation rested on.

## Why it still matters

- **Notation:** Leibniz's *d* and ∫ are what most of the world writes today. MacTutor credits his formalism as vital to later progress.
- **Culture:** The dispute is a standard example of how priority, publication, and national pride can collide in science.

## Worth knowing

Both built on earlier work (Barrow, Fermat, Cavalieri, and others). Some of their results had been found long before: Archimedes summed thin slices in the 3rd century BCE (see [[Archimedes Palimpsest]]), and the [[Kerala School]] had series for sine, cosine, and π by about 1400. Neither Newton nor Leibniz knew of the Kerala work, as far as the surviving evidence shows. Neither made calculus rigorous. Leibniz never thought of the derivative as a limit; that came later, and full rigor took until the 19th century.
