# Archimedes Palimpsest

**Hook:** Archimedes' notes on how he *found* his results about curved shapes survive in only one copy. A medieval scribe scraped it off to write a prayer book on top, and in March 2026 a lost page turned up in a French museum.

## The short version

- **The book:** a 10th-century Greek copy of several Archimedes treatises, almost certainly made in Constantinople. Before 1229 its pages were scraped and reused for a prayer book. That reuse is what makes it a **palimpsest**.
- **The treasure:** *The Method of Mechanical Theorems*, known from no other copy in any language. In it, Archimedes explains his private method for finding areas and volumes. He imagines a shape sliced into lines or thin pieces and **balanced on a lever** against a shape he already understands. He then proved the results rigorously by other means, the **[[Glossary#Method of exhaustion|method of exhaustion]]**.
- **Why calculus people care:** slicing a shape into infinitely many pieces and adding them back up is the idea behind the [[Glossary#Integral|integral]]. *Science News* (2007) said the treatise "could be considered the earliest known work on calculus". Rigor, general rules, and notation came much later.

## The timeline

- **1906:** Danish scholar **J. L. Heiberg** identifies the Archimedes text under the prayers and photographs it.
- **20th century:** the book disappears into a private collection in France. Forged religious paintings are added over some pages.
- **1998:** after export clearance in 1996, it sells at Christie's for about $2 million and goes to the **Walters Art Museum** in Baltimore for conservation and study.
- **Early 2000s:** **multispectral imaging** (photographs at many wavelengths of light) and X-ray fluorescence recover text Heiberg could not read.
- **March 2026:** CNRS researcher **Victor Gysembergh** identifies a leaf missing since Heiberg's day at the Musée des Beaux-Arts in **Blois**, matching it against Heiberg's 1906 photos. It holds part of *On the Sphere and Cylinder* (Book I, Propositions 39-41), not *The Method*. One side is covered by a 20th-century painting of Daniel and the lions. CNRS says new imaging is planned, subject to permission.

## The infinity claim (an interpretation, debated)

Stanford historian **Reviel Netz** reads Proposition 14 of *The Method* as Archimedes counting two infinite collections and treating them as equal in number. If that is right, it is a use of **actual infinity** about two millennia before set theory. Netz himself told *Science News* that "the argument is definitely not completely valid". Treat it as one scholar's reading of a hard-to-read text, not settled history.
