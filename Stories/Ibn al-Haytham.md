# Ibn al-Haytham

**Hook:** Around the year 1000, the "father of optics" found the volume of a sphere by stacking thin cylinders and adding up squares. It is the same squeeze that defines an integral today, about 650 years before Newton and Leibniz.

## Who

**Abu Ali al-Hasan ibn al-Haytham** (c. 965 to c. 1040), known in Latin Europe as **Alhazen**, was born in Basra and worked in Cairo. He is best known for his seven-volume *Book of Optics*. It was translated into Latin and influenced European physics. MacTutor's profile (by Heinz Klaus Strick) credits him with more than 200 works.

## The calculus-like part

- **Sums of powers.** Using a rectangle diagram, he derived the formula for 1² + 2² + ... + *n*². By reinterpreting the same picture, he reached sums of cubes and, per Strick, fourth, fifth, and sixth powers. Sums like these are exactly what is needed to add up thin slices.
- **The sphere, by slices.** In his *Treatise on the Volume of the Sphere*, he cuts a hemisphere into equal-height layers. He traps it between a stack of cylinders just inside and a stack just outside, then uses his sum-of-squares formula to compare both stacks with the enclosing cylinder. Like [[Archimedes Palimpsest|Archimedes]], he finishes with a double contradiction: the hemisphere cannot be more than two-thirds of the cylinder, and it cannot be less. In modern terms, that is an upper and a lower [[Glossary#Riemann sum|Riemann sum]] squeezing ∫ (r² − x²) dx.
- **Paraboloids.** Histories of calculus also credit him with the volume of a paraboloid, which needs the fourth-power sums. This Atlas has not yet checked that treatise.

## Is it calculus?

Not in the Newton-Leibniz sense. Each solid needs its own argument, and there is no general rule linking areas and slopes (the [[Fundamental Theorem of Calculus]]). But the method of **slicing, summing, and squeezing** is the core idea of the integral, the same one Archimedes used with the [[Glossary#Method of exhaustion|method of exhaustion]].
