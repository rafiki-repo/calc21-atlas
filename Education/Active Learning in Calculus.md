# Active Learning in Calculus

**Hook:** What happens if you stop lecturing calculus and make students do the work in class? One university ran a randomized trial to find out, and published it in *Science*.

## The study

**"Establishing a new standard of care for calculus using trials with randomized student allocation"**, by Laird Kramer, Edgar Fuller, Charity Watson, Adam Castillo, Pablo Duran Oliva, and Geoff Potvin. *Science* 381 (2023), pp. 995-998. Funded by the US National Science Foundation.

- **Where:** Florida International University, a large Hispanic-Serving public university.
- **Design:** Calculus I students were **randomly assigned** to an active-learning section or a traditional lecture section. The analysis covers **811 students**, **32 sections**, **19 instructors**, and **three semesters** (fall 2018 to fall 2019).
- **What "active" meant:** small groups solving problems and explaining ideas to one another in class, while instructors asked probing questions and adjusted the pace. The lecture group worked through most of the material on their own outside class.

## What it found

- **Better scores on end-of-semester learning measures** in the active sections. The abstract reports "large effect sizes" each semester.
- **Pass rates about 11% higher**, as reported by FIU and in Kramer's summary for *The Conversation*. Neither says whether that is percentage points or a relative gain.
- **Gains across groups:** race and ethnicity, gender, major, and first-time and transfer students, according to the authors. EdSurge reports the biggest gains were among biology majors.

## The fine print

- **Instructors were not randomized**, only students. Jon Baron, a former chair of the National Board for Education Sciences, called the result "encouraging but less than definitive" for that reason (quoted by EdSurge). Kramer has said randomizing teachers would have brought in other biases.
- **One university.** The authors say they plan replications elsewhere, including in high schools.
- **The headcounts differ.** The public dataset says 1,019 students were randomly assigned, while the abstract analyzes 811. The dataset page does not explain the gap.
- **"33,000 more students passing a year"**, a figure in FIU's coverage, is an extrapolation from the 11% figure to roughly 300,000 US calculus students. It is not a measured result.
