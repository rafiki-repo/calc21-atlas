# What's New

Latest additions and changes to the Atlas, newest first. Back to [[Home]].

## 2026-11-26
- **The Riemann hypothesis, claimed.** New [[Open Problems]] note: [[Riemann Hypothesis]]. On Nov 20, [[Levent Alpoge|Levent Alpöge]] posted a claimed proof made "working with Claude": a four-page paper and about 17 million lines of Lean. A day later the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative certified the Lean proof. Mathematicians do not yet understand the argument, and nothing is peer reviewed. The note also covers two earlier 2026 steps: an Anthropic model's bound putting at least 67.2% of zeros on the critical line (Aug) and OpenAI's quasi-Riemann hypothesis (Oct).
- **2026's prizes in analysis.** [[Research Watch]] adds the 2026 Clay Research Awards (Orponen, Shmerkin, Wang, and Zahl for Kakeya and Furstenberg; Deng and Hani for Boltzmann) and the King Faisal Prize. New profile: [[Carlos Kenig]] (Chicago), who won that prize for nonlinear PDEs and was IMU president from 2019 to 2022.
- **Also:** [[Kakeya Conjecture]] adds its cousin, the Furstenberg set conjecture (proved in the plane by Ren and Wang, 2023 preprint). [[Proof Assistants and Lean]], [[AI as Research Collaborator]] (episode 6), [[Levent Alpoge]], and [[Basel Problem]] link to the new note. The [[Glossary]] adds *dispersive equation* and *Riemann zeta function*. New Trail 14 in [[Rabbit Holes]], "From π²/6 to the Riemann hypothesis."

## 2026-11-25
- **The software shelf.** New [[Resources]] note: [[Graphing and Computer Algebra Tools]] compares Desmos, GeoGebra, SageMath, SymPy, and Wolfram|Alpha: what each does, what it costs, and who runs it. Also: a built-in Desmos calculator comes with the calculator parts of the 2027 AP Calculus exams, and an Oct 2025 list of assets for sale in the insolvency of BYJU'S, GeoGebra's parent group, includes its GeoGebra stake.
- **A calculus with steps of 1.** New [[Frontiers]] note: [[Discrete Calculus]], where differences replace derivatives, sums replace integrals, and "falling powers" obey a power rule. It turns 1² + 2² + ... + *n*² into a one-line calculation.
- **Try a Putnam problem.** [[Putnam Competition]] now features 2025's A2: squeeze the sine arch between two parabolas (answer hidden).
- **Also:** [[Free Calculus Textbooks]] adds UBC's four-volume CLP Calculus. [[AP Calculus]] notes the built-in Desmos. The [[Glossary]] adds *computer algebra system* and *finite difference*. Trails 5 and 9 in [[Rabbit Holes]] get new stops.

## 2026-11-24
- **Do AI tutors teach?** New [[Education]] note: [[AI Tutors in Math Class]] collects five randomized studies. In a Turkish high school (*PNAS*, 2025), an open GPT-4 chatbot gave 48% higher practice scores and then 17% lower exam scores. A Harvard physics trial reported more than double the learning gains of active learning. Khanmigo in 18 Tennessee middle schools gave small gains, about the same as Khan Academy without the AI. No randomized trial in college calculus turned up yet; the closest is a 2026 Calculus I case study.
- **New application:** [[Physics-Informed Neural Networks]], which are trained to obey a differential equation. The note covers what they are good at (inverse problems, no grid) and the fine print (training can fail; they are usually slower and less accurate than classic solvers).
- **Updated:** [[Calculus and AI]] adds IntegralBench (the best of nine models solved about half of 317 hard definite integrals, in a 2025 preprint). The [[Glossary]] adds *automatic differentiation*. Trails 4, 8, and 13 in [[Rabbit Holes]] get new stops.
- **Also:** the [README](README.md) now links to the web version at calc21.com/atlas (thanks to the calc21.com team).

## 2026-11-23
- **Calculus class, examined.** Two new [[Education]] notes. [[AP Calculus]] covers the AB and BC exams: shorter multiple-choice sections from May 2027, pass rates from 2002 to 2026 (BC takers nearly quadrupled), an unexplained 2024 jump in AB scores, and the new AP Precalculus. [[Active Learning in Calculus]] covers a 2023 *Science* trial at FIU: 811 students randomly assigned to group work or lectures, with better results for group work. Its fine print: instructors were not randomized, and it was run at one university.
- **Updated:** [[Widening Access to Calculus]] adds California data (calculus takers fell from 17.8% to 14.2% between 2015 and 2024, and fewer schools offer it). [[Calculus or Statistics Debate]] adds UC's Dec 2025 statement that its math rules "have not changed for over 16 years."
- **Also:** the [[Glossary]] adds the *extreme value theorem*. The [[Terence Tao]] note gets a formatting fix (thanks to the calc21.com team). New Trail 13 in [[Rabbit Holes]].

## 2026-11-21
- **Two blow-up hunters.** New profiles of [[Tristan Buckmaster]] (NYU) and [[Javier Gomez-Serrano|Javier Gómez-Serrano]] (Brown). Buckmaster first showed that rough fluid solutions can be wildly non-unique (2019 Clay Research Award). Gómez-Serrano built a career on computer-assisted proofs. Together, since 2022, they have used neural networks to search for fluid singularities. None of those candidates is proved yet. Buckmaster's forced-Euler result is still a claim under review.
- **Catalan's constant:** a Sep 2026 preprint claims it is irrational. A follow-up note reports a gap in the proof. [[Irrationality of Zeta Values]] now covers the dispute; the question is still treated as open.
- **Fixed:** [[Cordoba and Martinez-Zoroa]] now gives Luis Martínez-Zoroa's confirmed post (CUNEF Universidad, after a Basel postdoc).
- **Also:** the [[Glossary]] adds *singularity* and *weak solution*. [[People]] adds a line on Thomas Hou. Trail 12 in [[Rabbit Holes]] gets two new stops.

## 2026-11-20
- **Two open problems on the move.** [[Irrationality of Zeta Values]]: a Sep 2026 preprint by Aabir Fauzan claims that ζ(5) = 1 + 1/2⁵ + 1/3⁵ + ... is irrational. That would be the first named odd zeta value since Apéry's ζ(3) in 1978. Lean and Isabelle versions already check it, and peer review has not happened yet. Euler's constant γ is still a mystery. The [[Moving Sofa Problem]]: Jineon Baek's 119-page proof that Gerver's 1992 sofa is the largest that fits around a corner, uses Green's theorem and no computer. It is reported to be under review at *Annals*.
- **New person:** [[Steven Strogatz]], author of *Infinite Powers* and host of *The Joy of Why*.
- **Updated:** [[Research Watch]] (two new rows), [[Proof Assistants and Lean]] (the ζ(5) formalization), [[Basel Problem]] (what happens at odd powers). The [[Glossary]] adds *Euler's constant* and *irrational number*. Trails 2 and 3 in [[Rabbit Holes]] get new stops.
